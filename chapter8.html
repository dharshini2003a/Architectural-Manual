8. Championing Responsible Healthcare by Creating Energy Efficient Sustainable Hospital Infrastructure

Contributed by Smart Joules

The global healthcare sector's climate footprint is equivalent to 4.4 percent of global emissions, contributing around 2 gigatonnes of carbon dioxide into the environment. This is bigger than the carbon footprint of other industries like aviation and shipping, and equivalent to 514 coal-fired power plants. Reports also say that if healthcare were a country, it would be the fifth largest emitter on Earth.

At the same time, with 0.6 beds per 1,000 population against a global average of 2.9 and 4.3 in China, India's healthcare sector is poised to grow rapidly. It is therefore imperative to practice Efficiency First while designing, building and operating hospitals to meet India's burning need. The good news is that simple and proven methods to make substantial reductions in carbon impact exist today, and that most sustainable options are also the most economical.

10 Practical Tips While Planning Infrastructure Development

Here are the 10 tips across procurement methods, design practices, equipment selection, O&M practices and culture, distilled from a decade-long experience of reducing carbon impact by 29% with above-market returns on investment across 49 large Indian hospitals.

1. Procurement

a. Procurement move to performance contracting models

Since individual institutions are unlikely to be subject matter experts in energy, one of the most potent tools at leadership's disposal is to move towards performance contracting related to their main energy-consuming systems. For instance, if one insists on buying cooling at a fixed price per ton-hour rather than separately buying design services, equipment such as chillers, pumps and cooling towers, and O&M services to run the infrastructure, the industry would naturally gravitate towards optimising energy efficiency to minimize cooling cost.

b. Choose lowest lifecycle, not capital costs

Start asking your procurement teams to present comparative analysis of various capital equipment in terms of total cost of ownership over its useful life, including capital costs, maintenance costs and resource (energy, water, chemicals) costs. Often you will find that the lowest lifecycle cost equipment is also the most sustainable option, since the largest component of lifecycle cost of ownership is not the equipment itself, but the cost of energy it consumes over time.

2. Design

a. Adopt adaptive thermal comfort

The single largest energy consumer in any hospital is the cooling system. Cooling system designers must apply the concept of adaptive thermal comfort, which recognises that facilities needn't maintain fixed temperature conditions throughout the year since people's preferences change with seasonal changes and external weather conditions. It allows facilities to achieve higher energy efficiency and more comfortable indoor climates for occupants, while enhancing chances for top green building ratings and boosting overall facility value.

b. Choose passive and water-based cooling

Passive cooling technologies and practices harness natural processes to maintain comfortable indoor temperatures. If practised at the design stage, they can minimise reliance on mechanical cooling systems, achieving a lower carbon footprint, lower up-front and operational costs, enhanced thermal comfort, greater space availability, and higher facility value. Passive cooling includes natural ventilation, shading and sun control techniques, using materials with high thermal mass (e.g., concrete, brick) that absorb and store heat during the day and release it at night in the building structure, interior walls and floors, cool/green roofs, evaporative cooling, structure cooling, optimal building orientation, etc.

If there is still a need for mechanical cooling to cover remaining loads or for dehumidification, one should rely on water-based systems (water-cooled chillers, radiant cooling) vs. air-based ones (air-cooled chillers, VRVs, split ACs) since they offer 40-50% higher energy efficiency levels and much lower lifecycle costs of ownership.

c. Choose electric vs. solid, liquid or gaseous fuels

Hospitals often rely on solid (coal), liquid (furnace oil, diesel, LPG) and gaseous (PNG, CNG) fossil fuels to fulfil their heating requirements for kitchens, laundries, sterilization departments and domestic hot water needs (bathing). Electrification of heating by using heat pumps and electric boilers offers a profitable decarbonization alternative, especially since the carbon intensity of electricity overall is rapidly declining with the widespread adoption of renewable energy.

3. Equipment

a. Ask for premium efficiency models

Designers, OEMs and contractors routinely choose lower efficiency models as their default choice since they are more mainstream and often slightly cheaper in capital cost. However, high efficiency models exist for almost any appliance or equipment that one needs to install in a hospital, including lighting (LEDs), fans (BLDC fans), motors (IE5 motors), cooling towers (low approach cooling towers), refrigerators (5-star rated), etc. Asking partners to opt for the highest efficiency models by default will lead to more sustainable and, over the long term, more economical and reliable infrastructure.

b. Choose variable speed equipment

Hospital needs are dynamic in nature. For instance, the need for cooling, hot water and steam changes within a day and across seasons with changes in occupancy and weather. Therefore, the equipment and systems that serve them need the ability to also calibrate their output to maintain high efficiency levels under various loading scenarios. This necessitates the need to aggressively opt for variable speed drives (VSDs / VFDs) in all the large (>1.5 kW) blowers (AHUs), pumps (raw water, STP, HVAC) and compressors (chillers, air compressors) installed in our hospitals.

VFDs typically have very short payback periods (<1 year) since the ratio of reduction of power input to reduction of system output follows a cubic law — a 30% reduction in air flow saves 65% of power from an AHU.

4. Operations and Maintenance

a. Utilize 100% of STP water on-site

Most hospitals spend massive amounts of money on meeting their water needs through fresh water from municipal connections, tankers or borewell systems, while at the same time discharging inadequately treated water to their surroundings. Properly designed and well-operated sewage treatment plants can allow hospitals to reuse close to 100% of their wastewater on-site in applications such as cooling, flushing and gardening. This leads to minimal fresh water costs as well as better alignment with existing and future regulations around wastewater disposal. Companies such as Digital Paani, Boson White Water and ECOSTP provide comprehensive solutions for hospitals' needs.

b. Practice continuous monitoring and reporting against relevant KPIs

The key to successful implementation of any decarbonization or energy efficiency drive lies in formulating a set of compelling key performance indicators (KPIs) and instituting systems to continuously monitor and report against these KPIs. For hospitals, these key metrics include kWh/bed, kW/ton for cooling, running hours of key energy-guzzling equipment, set points and area temperatures maintained, kWh consumed every month by the top 10 energy consuming equipment, etc. Infrastructure to measure, record, store, display, analyse and report such data is extremely cost effective today and must be opted for by default by hospitals.

5. Fostering a Culture of Sustainable Practices

Moving sustainability from the basement to the boardroom

Perhaps the most important initiative of all is to culturally practice sustainability, starting with leadership teams. If leadership starts asking the right questions to the right people at the right time, results will follow. For instance, sustainability metrics around energy and water are reported every month by site engineering teams to unit heads, by unit heads to corporate leadership teams, and then in turn to the Board of Directors in companies such as KIMS and Aster. Other chains such as Apollo encourage their Chief Engineers to apply for national and international conferences and competitions around energy efficiency and sustainability, offering them a chance to enhance their knowledge and aggression levels with respect to sustainability.
