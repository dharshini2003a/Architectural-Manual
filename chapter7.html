Patient and staff safety considerations in the layout design of eye hospitals

A. Introduction

Though we don't immediately connect hospital buildings to patient and staff safety, in reality, buildings have a significant impact, both on safety and efficiency. This section brings out that connection explicitly and offers insights on how patient and staff safety can be factored in, both while planning for new hospitals as well as how existing building spaces can be made safer.

People visit hospitals to get well. However, the potential for falls, accidents, cross infection and exposure to hazards are high in hospitals due to the inherent complexity of hospital buildings, the high number of first-time visitors, their age, anxiety, disabilities, and unfamiliar equipment and procedures. Good building design that considers these would maximise physical safety and give psychological reassurance to patients and comfort and ease of working for hospital staff.

Unlike equipment or staffing, buildings can't be easily altered or enlarged for a multitude of reasons, ranging from feasibility to regulatory challenges. Thus, it is important that right at the planning stage we consider two high-level factors – anticipated patient volumes at full capacity and workflow based on the clinical protocols. This consideration applies to outpatient visits, surgeries and other treatment procedures.

B. Safety considerations in building design

Hospital buildings tend to be custom-built to accommodate required clinical procedures as well as the promoter's preferences. Patients who are already vulnerable, often find the hospital experience intimidating, especially during the initial visits, due to unfamiliarity with the process and flow. Multiple entries (Emergency, OP, IP) with signage or lack of it, disorienting patients, is just one example.

An understanding of the workflow requirements, appreciation of patient needs and factoring in anticipated growth of patient volumes, will all help in designing an efficient and safe hospital. Such understanding can also help make necessary changes in existing facilities, to enhance patient safety (ref: 2). In all this we need to recognize two important factors – first, by design in an eye hospital most patients tend to be visually challenged and elderly and second, some patients are likely to be the source of cross infections, due to other illnesses (ref: 1). The following are some of the more important considerations.

1. Building configuration and workflow

The layout of various clinical and patient-related administrative services should match the workflow. The space allocation for each of the services should reflect the patient volumes. When this isn't done, it results in bottlenecks, increased travel distances and criss-cross movement of patients and staff. The greater the movement, the more the scope for falls and accidents, thereby resulting in lower efficiency.

2. Planning for growth in patient load and changes in technology

As a hospital's reputation grows, so does the number of patients as well as the staff and equipment. New equipment and procedures would also require additional space. Under these circumstances, additional spaces for outpatient services and operating rooms must be contiguous to existing ones. This would ensure that the building layout continues to mirror the workflow (ref: 3).

This requires some foresight matched by appropriate provisioning in the plans. In the absence of this, it results in ad-hoc expansion which often duplicates equipment, staffing and a lot more movement. It must also be appreciated that insufficient and improper space allocation will result in overcrowding and compromised safety and caregiving processes.

C. Safety considerations in routine work (ref: 4)

The safety considerations can be broadly grouped into two, as in everyday work and rare, but with catastrophic consequences. While some of these may be mandated by regulations, it is in the best interest to address them proactively. The following are lists of safety measures, further grouped under broad safety concerns.

1.0 Everyday safety

This relates to potential safety issues likely to occur every day as part of the routine caregiving process. There is a tendency in all of us to get used to challenges and shortcomings and figure out ways to work around them, rather than fixing them. So, it is important to do periodic audits, preferably by external persons. The following could serve as a guide or checklist, for existing or new buildings being planned.

1.1 Preventing falls from heights, slips, and trips

  • Guard rails and barriers of sufficient height and strength in both interior and exterior spaces should be provided.
  • Interior and exterior floor surfaces should have sufficient traction to prevent slips and falls.
  • Clear visibility with good lighting at ramps, stairs and corridors.
  • Avoid sudden changes in floor levels at unexpected locations.
  • Wherever water is used, ensure it drains readily and doesn't create slippery surfaces.

1.2 Preventing collisions

  • All clear glass partitions should have visual barrier strips or designs to prevent anyone from mistakenly trying to walk through.
  • Use swing doors rather than sliding doors. People in a hurry tend to assume doors swing open and walk into sliding doors. It is also easier to operate swing doors while pushing wheelchairs and trolleys.
  • Use appropriate handles to open doors – a handle if a door has to be pulled open and a horizontal bar when a door has to be pushed open.
  • Where two directional movements are expected, use double swing doors with view panels.
  • The speed and force of auto-close doors should be calibrated to avoid accidents.
  • Avoid projections and low-hanging sign boards.
  • Avoid doors opening into corridors, ramps and stairs.

1.3 Prevention of workplace issues

  • Task lighting should be considered. Avoid glare on computer screens and worktops from bright lights and windows. Adjustable blinds can help in controlling glare from windows.
  • Good lighting ensures that labels and reports are properly read and reduces the chances of errors.
  • A noise-free environment enhances concentration and productivity. Communication between patient and caregiver is clearer, less ambiguous and more effective in spaces with low ambient noise.
  • Plan the internal layout of the room – position furniture, equipment and cables to enhance ease of work and safety.
  • Position equipment and power outlets appropriately. At all costs avoid cables snaking across floors or hanging in the air.
  • All hazardous equipment should be housed and used as recommended, with proper safety warnings posted.
  • Appropriate safety gear should be given to staff working with potentially hazardous equipment like lasers.
  • Good washrooms and break areas away from patients are often missed but essential.

1.4 Adequate ventilation and good indoor air quality

  • Maximize natural and good cross ventilation. Courtyards bring natural ventilation and lighting into deep hospital buildings.
  • Courtyards also act as social and spillover spaces, a great reference point for navigation and seamlessly connecting the outdoors with the indoors. Courtyards can expand capacities during emergencies as well.
  • Where natural ventilation is not feasible, plan on mechanical ventilation as per standards and work area requirements.
  • Hospital air conditioning needs are very specific and may not be met by domestic units. Consult experts for proper air conditioning. It's also important to have optimal relative humidity.
  • Use materials and furnishings that are low emitters of indoor air contaminants and volatile organic compounds (VOCs).

1.5 Electrical safety

  • All electrical installations should strictly be done as per the respective country's electrical safety codes.
  • Good earthing increases the safety as well as the life of sensitive electrical equipment.
  • Integrate safety devices in the electrical system to prevent short circuits and overloads.
  • Label all electrical panels. Have circuit diagrams displayed in panels for quick reference during emergencies.
  • Use colour coding of switches and power outlets to identify UPS power, power with standby and power without standby.
  • If wetness is noticed in and around an electrical installation, get it attended to on priority.
  • Replace immediately any exposed or frayed cables.
  • Do not plug in multiple pieces of equipment on the same outlet.

1.6 Hazardous materials and waste handling

  • Hospital processes may use and/or generate hazardous materials. Identify possible hazardous materials that need to be handled and lay out clear guidelines (SOPs) to isolate, remove and manage such materials.
  • Separate holding/storage areas must be planned for hazardous materials away from public use spaces.
  • Waste generated in hospitals, including contaminated materials and sharps, requires careful handling. Source segregation helps in proper management of waste and recycling. Design locations for collection bins in all areas of waste generation that ensure easy deposition and safe removal.
  • Disposal of contaminated waste should abide by local regulations, and in their absence, by practices advocated by WHO.

2.0 Rare occurrences

Rare occurrences, as the name implies, may never happen — but when they do, the impact is often catastrophic. So, it is all the more important that there is adequate preparation and periodic mock drills and checks to ensure the readiness or fitness of the precautionary measures in place. This should be done even when not mandated. The following is a listing of potential rare occurrences and the required preparedness.

2.1 Fire protection

  • Modern hospital buildings use electro-mechanical systems and fossil fuels which increase the possibility of fire hazards. Fire protection engineers must be involved in all aspects of design to ensure the protection of human life and to reduce potential losses to equipment and property from fire.
  • The building and fire codes must be adhered to.
  • Exit paths should be identified and kept clear of any obstruction at all times.
  • Areas of refuge – a place to safely gather to be rescued by fire personnel – in multi-storey buildings should be designated and maintained.
  • Proper fire detection, alarm and suppression systems can save life and property.
  • Emergency lighting and power systems should function and be kept in readiness at all times through regular checks and maintenance.
  • It is important to have fire-related information and signage displayed in local languages.
  • Regular fire drills and training of staff should be given priority.

2.2 Earthquakes

  • Most national building codes specify the highest degree of earthquake resistance for hospital buildings.
  • Hospitals not only have to be safe for their patients and staff but also should be able to function fully to provide relief to all affected in the area in the aftermath of earthquakes.
  • The integrity of the building to withstand earthquakes needs to be taken care of by experienced structural engineers and proper construction.
  • All large and heavy equipment should be properly anchored at all times to ensure that they do not cause damage during earthquakes.
  • Emergency preparedness for earthquakes should be part of the safety protocol of hospitals.

2.3 Storms, heavy rain, snow, flooding, landslides, etc.

  • Many natural and seasonal occurrences could create safety issues for hospitals.
  • Good site selection – higher ground, away from flowing water bodies, well-drained – helps to a great degree in mitigating possible safety issues during such natural calamities.
  • One may not have the luxury of an ideal site. Good engineering design can then greatly reduce damage from such hazards.
  • All equipment and systems meant to work during such eventualities, like storm drains and pumps, should be in working condition at all times and located in areas unlikely to be affected by flooding, etc.

2.4 Security from human threats

  • Security risks could range from pilferage and theft to vandalism and terrorist attacks. Hospitals are open to everyone and this makes them vulnerable to threats. However, the number of entrances could be planned, balancing functional needs, efficiency and security concerns.
  • Surveillance systems should be planned without compromising privacy.
  • The hospital design should be such that patients' and visitors' movement can be contained based on needs, and passing through unnecessary areas is avoided.
  • Areas of minimal movement like parking garages and storage areas should be closely monitored.

D. Good building design

Safety considerations in hospital buildings can go hand-in-hand to create a friendly environment and an enjoyable experience.

Courtyards, skylights, corridors leading towards points of visual interest/views, designed niches for parking stretchers and equipment and recesses, and the use of colour and texture serve functional needs and enhance the spatial experience. Modular planning and standardisation could bring easy comprehension of a hospital layout and provide flexibility, with minimal physical alteration.

Great hospital designs are people-centric.
