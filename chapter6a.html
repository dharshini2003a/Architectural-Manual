Outpatient care – design considerations and guidelines

Building layout design depends on the available space, service mix and planned volumes. Hence, providing a prescribed layout design for an eye hospital is not feasible. Instead, this architecture guide focuses on preferred practices and considerations for designing an eye hospital. To make it practical, detailing is provided at a unit level. It is hoped that the planners would find this approach useful for the discussions to design the layout.

To help with this, three sets of resources are included in this section:

  • High-level layouts (PDFs) - as models to develop a better understanding of the flows, set-up, adjacencies, etc.
  • Layouts at the unit level (PDFs) of consultation rooms, refraction lanes, investigation areas, etc.
  • Detailed checklists – The checklists in the MS-Excel version are designed as tools for the planners to play with. The versions can be downloaded to work out the requirements (space, flows, electrical and waterline layouts, IT networks, etc.).

The recent technological advancements in ophthalmic clinical sciences have created an opportunity to provide comprehensive eye care allowing better diagnosis and widening the scope of treatment even for advanced eye conditions in both the anterior and posterior segments. This is further augmented by the integration of artificial intelligence, the Internet of Things (IoT), imaging technologies, etc. All these significantly impact the design and space requirements of eye hospitals with the treatment practices in eye care becoming more ambulatory.

Thus the need to allocate a larger space of 50% + of the total built-up area for outpatient services has become a new norm. This section will explore the impact of outpatient flow on space requirements and layout design in eye clinics and provide best practices for optimal space planning and patient flow.

1. Service levels of care delivery

Secondary level:

Secondary level focuses primarily on refractive errors and cataract surgery. Diagnostics would include assessment of both ophthalmic and systemic conditions. Space required for diagnostics and investigations in secondary eye care would typically be less than what is required to accommodate doctors consulting rooms, refraction lanes, waiting areas, counselling, etc. A few other practices do exist:

  • Some hospitals may be equipped to carry out comprehensive diagnostics to assess both anterior and posterior segments as well as the medical management (lasers for yag capsulotomy/glaucoma/diabetic retinopathy)
  • Some may run specific clinic days through visiting consultants who may provide surgical services for subspeciality conditions on specific periodicity every month

Tertiary Level:

In addition to the above, hospitals are equipped and resourced adequately to provide comprehensive care for patients routinely. The space planning for outpatient services is determined by the patient volumes. For example, high-volume settings with significant subspeciality volumes could justify standalone subspecialty clinics. In low-patient volume settings, it would be more pragmatic to have a common investigation area, connected to various subspecialty consulting rooms since they use the same diagnostic equipment (eg. OCT); secondly, they are expensive and third, require an additional number of skilled staff to operate them which would add to the fixed costs especially if the volumes do not justify the additional workforce.

The ancillary clinical services such as optical services, pharmacy, biochemistry and microbiology labs, and examination by physicians (to assess systemic fitness) would require additional space if the eye hospitals decide to offer them inhouse – Making these services available under one roof provides a one-stop service for patients resulting in better compliance to treatment and patient experience.

Patient flow in a typical outpatient clinic

A typical outpatient clinic would consist of the following:

  • Patient registration & cash counters
  • Waiting for areas for different stations
  • Refraction lanes
  • Consultation suites
  • Investigations
  • Outpatient procedure area
  • Counselling (to improve service uptake)
  • Processing patients with insurance where applicable
  • Dispensing outlets such as optical, pharmacy, etc.

2. Layout design of an outpatient consultation area

The layout of an outpatient clinic is heavily influenced by the workflow protocol followed for the eye exams, which typically includes four key components:

  1. (a) Taking history
  2. (b) Assessing vision, performing undilated/dilated refraction
  3. (c) Assessments such as measuring blood pressure and intraocular pressure, OCT, fundus imaging, the field of vision assessment
  4. (d) Consultation with an ophthalmologist

Diagnostics for advanced eye conditions will be carried out if suggested by the doctor. Two types of workflow/patient flow are widely practised:

(i) Multi-station workflow

In low and middle-income countries, the multi-station workflow is followed predominantly which requires patients to move from one station to another while the corresponding staff remains in their designated stations to perform their functions.

(ii) Stationary model

The stationary model allows for carrying out common assessments and doctors' consultations in fully equipped consultation suites. In this model, patients stay in the same consultation room while the doctors and staff may rotate between exam rooms to perform their clinical duties.

In both models, consultation rooms are often connected through service corridors to enable staff to move freely without disrupting patient traffic.

Though the overall size of an outpatient clinic may not vary between the two models, there will be noticeable differences in the layout design as well as the patient throughput. These differences can include room sizes and layouts, corridors designed to facilitate staff movement, patient waiting areas, equipment needs, and more. The decision to adopt a particular model is based on several factors, such as the cost, available staffing, and local cultural preferences of the community being served.

3.Investigation & procedure area

Ophthalmic care involves two levels of investigation.

(i) Routine procedures

The routine procedures that are typically carried out as part of a regular consultation process such as refraction, measuring intraocular pressure and blood pressure for patients aged 40 and above, checking duct patency (applicable South East Asia), and biometry for individuals undergoing cataract surgery.

(ii) Advanced diagnostics

Advanced diagnostics, such as optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), fundus imaging and Visual field analyzers, are indicated for patients with sub-specialty eye conditions.

(iii) Procedure room(s)

Procedure room(s) to facilitate clinical procedures, performing laser treatments, a sterile area to administer intra-vitreal injections, foreign body/suture removals, etc.

Key design consideration: Placing the diagnostics and procedure rooms as adjacencies to the consultation rooms, along with sufficient waiting areas, is crucial for maintaining a smooth and efficient patient flow.

Section B

Preferred practices in designing an outpatient clinic

1. Standardized room sizes and grid model

Adopting standardized room sizes would allow positioning the columns so that they don't come inside the rooms and thus give flexibility in room layout. This design involves constructing rooms with the same length but varying widths. This approach optimizes available space and allows for the placement of load-bearing reinforced concrete pillars without obstructing patient flow or examination areas. 2. Construction type

To accommodate increasing patient volumes and new clinical and support services, a well-run eye hospital is likely to undergo renovations. Opting for aluminium/plywood partitions or similar materials instead of walled rooms provides greater flexibility to redesign the space when changes are necessary. This approach makes renovations a cost-effective and quicker exercise. Therefore, the type of construction chosen for the hospital should take into consideration the need for future flexibility and adaptability.

3. Standardized workflow protocol

A streamlined linear flow of patients throughout the system is enabled by a standardized workflow protocol. This then becomes part of the design consideration. Therefore, it is highly advisable to standardize the clinical processes before consulting an architect.

4. Considerations for each room/space

As applicable:

i. Power outlets based on source

  • Grid/Generator power: Standard supply for regular electric fixtures
  • Uninterrupted Power Supply (UPS): To protect ophthalmic equipment, computers, and IT networks from voltage fluctuations, UPS is essential, particularly in low-middle-income countries where power supply could be erratic.

ii. Water pipelines and drainage

Handwashing facilities in the consultation rooms to reduce cross-infections.

iii. LAN/Wireless connectivity

For patient care management and the use of electronic medical records.

iv. Flexible lighting and temperature settings

Arrangements to meet the requirements for diagnostics as well as interventions.

5. Patient privacy

The design layout should prioritize patient privacy during the consultation process as per the local cultural preferences.

6. Wider doors and corridors

Consider designing wider doors at least in a few rooms and corridors to enable unrestricted movement of wheelchairs and stretchers.

7. Wheelchair/stretcher alcove

Provision of wheelchair/stretcher alcove in an accessible area.

8. Accessibility for patients with disabilities

Although patients with disabilities may be a minority among the outpatients who visit an eye hospital or clinic, it is crucial to provide them with equal access to facilities and services. Therefore, it is important to create disabled-friendly areas that offer wheelchair-accessible toilets, safety railings, and special arrangements for patient movement across different sections of the hospital. These considerations will help ensure that patients with disabilities can experience the same level of care and access as other patients.

9. Anti-skid flooring

Anti-skid flooring to reduce chances of a fall (for more details on patient safety, please refer to the section-Patient & staff safety).

10. Provision of utilities

Ensure provision of utilities such as toilets for staff and patients, water points, cafeteria, etc.

11. Fire safety

It is critical to consider the provision of fire safety (sprinklers, fire extinguishers, smoke detectors etc.) in adherence to local regulations in different locations of OPD.

12. Communication systems

A public address system and CCTV network that enables hospital-wide communication/providing instructions is desirable and should be included while planning the electrical network.

13. Multiple cash counters

Setting up multiple cash counters can eliminate unnecessary movement of patients/attendants and also reduce the waiting time. This is applicable either in the high volume settings or different services operational on different floors.

14. Separate toilets

Planning for separate toilets for staff and patients in the OPD area.
